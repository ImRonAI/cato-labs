## Section #approach: Why twenty kilometres.
02   Approach

### Why twenty kilometres.

- 400 kmLow Earth orbit. Fast, far, and visible to everyone.

- 100 kmThe Kármán line.

- 20 – 26 kmThe stratosphere. Where we hold station.

- 12 kmCommercial air corridors.

- 0 – 10 kmWeather.

Above weather and traffic, below orbit. Thin enough air to fly on sunlight; thick enough to steer. A platform here sees a region the way a satellite does, but it can stay, and it can be brought home.

Persistence

#### Months, not passes.

A satellite revisits. A stratollite remains. Continuous custody of a region changes what can be noticed.

Autonomy

#### Quiet flight computers.

Radiation-hardened avionics hold position through the stratospheric wind cycle without a hand on the controls.

Links

#### Light, not radio.

Optical inter-platform links carry data between airframes and to ground without broadcasting the fact.

### 01   SystemV-9 SentinelLow-observable observation stratollite

Built to arrive quickly and then be forgotten about: a quiet airframe that keeps a radar and a camera over the same stretch of sea for three days at a time.

- RolePersistent SAR and EO/IR observation
- Service ceiling≈ 20.7 km (68,000 ft)
- Endurance72 hours
- Modular payload450 kg, multi-sensor pods
- Primary sensorsX-band synthetic-aperture radar; electro-optical
- Inter-platform linkOptical laser terminal
- AirframeLow-observable composite

#### What it is for

The Sentinel is the system we send first. It climbs to the stratosphere in hours, opens a wide synthetic-aperture radar swath over a maritime or littoral region, and holds it there while longer-duration platforms are positioned. It is also the airframe of choice for shorter, higher-tempo tasks where a camera and a radar need to be in one place now.

#### How it flies

A composite airframe with a small radar cross-section and a low thermal signature, flown autonomously from climb to recovery. The payload bay accepts pods rather than fixed sensors, so a Sentinel can carry radar on one sortie and electro-optical or signals equipment on the next.

#### What it carries home

Imagery and radar products are passed over the optical inter-platform link to a Helios Wing or directly to ground, so nothing is broadcast on radio frequencies that would announce the aircraft. Data arrives processed, geolocated and time-stamped.

### 02   SystemV-14 Helios WingSolar-electric high-altitude pseudo-satellite

Fifty-two metres of wing, flying on light. It takes a position above a region in the stratospheric wind-null layer and simply does not leave.

- RolePersistent wide-area sensing and communications relay
- Operating altitude≈ 23.2 km (76,000 ft), continuous
- Station-keeping90 days and more
- Wingspan52.4 m, gallium-arsenide solar skin
- PowerSolar by day; solid-state battery reserve by night
- PayloadWide-area signals sensing; communications mesh node
- Inter-platform linkOptical laser trunk

#### What it is for

The Helios Wing is a pseudo-satellite: a platform that offers what a geostationary satellite offers over a region, but from twenty-three kilometres rather than thirty-six thousand, and with the option of coming home. It is the backbone of persistent observation and the relay node that keeps a region connected.

#### How it stays

The wing is built for the layer of the stratosphere where winds are weakest. By day the solar skin powers flight and charges the battery; by night the aircraft glides slowly on reserve, losing little altitude. The result is a position held for three months at a time, with recovery and relaunch to extend it.

#### Why it matters

Persistence changes the question. A satellite tells you what a place looked like at 10:42. A Helios Wing tells you what changed since yesterday, and the day before, and lets you ask the next question while it is still overhead.

### 03   SystemV-22 ApexUncrewed hypersonic suborbital vehicle

The hours matter sometimes. The Apex exists for repositioning sensors and command links across a region faster than anything that stays inside the atmosphere.

- RoleRapid repositioning; survivable command-and-control relay
- CruiseMach 6 class
- Operating corridor≈ 26 km (85,000 ft) and above
- RepositioningUnder 3.5 hours to any region
- PropulsionDual-mode scramjet with magnetohydrodynamic flow control
- Thermal protectionCeramic-matrix leading edges with closed-loop cooling
- AvionicsRadiation-hardened, hardware root of trust

#### What it is for

Most of what we do is slow on purpose. The Apex is the exception. When a region needs sensing or a command link in hours rather than days, it carries a relay or a sensor package across the stratosphere at hypersonic speed and either holds a high suborbital arc or hands the task to a stratollite on arrival.

#### How it flies

A dual-mode engine takes the vehicle from ramjet speeds into scramjet cruise. Electromagnetic flow control conditions the air entering the engine without moving parts. Leading edges are ceramic-matrix composite, cooled by a closed loop that recovers waste heat as electrical power for the avionics.

#### What we do not say

We publish the class of performance and the role. We do not publish operational detail, and we describe this system as what it is: a relay and repositioning vehicle, not a weapon.

### I   Programme · V-9 Sentinel · V-14 Helios WingPersistent observation

Watching a region for weeks at a time, from above the weather, so that change can be seen as it happens rather than reconstructed afterwards.

#### The problem

Satellites pass. Aircraft return to base. Between those passes, things move: ships change course, ice shifts, a coastline floods. Observation that stops and starts leaves the gaps where the questions are.

#### What we deliver

A Sentinel to open the watch and a Helios Wing to keep it. Radar and electro-optical products geolocated and time-stamped, delivered over optical links so the platforms themselves stay quiet. Continuous custody of a region for weeks or months.

#### Typical applications

- Maritime domain awareness over shipping lanes and exclusive economic zones
- Polar and sea-ice monitoring where satellite coverage is sparse
- Wildfire, flood and disaster-response imaging for civil authorities
- Border and littoral surveillance for allied ministries

### II   Programme · V-14 Helios WingResilient communications

A relay node that sits above a region for months and keeps its command and data links alive when the satellites it would normally use are degraded or denied.

#### The problem

Modern operations assume connectivity. When satellite links are jammed, spoofed or simply oversubscribed, a region goes quiet at exactly the moment it should not.

#### What we deliver

Helios Wings positioned as stratospheric relay nodes. Each carries a communications mesh payload and optical trunks to neighbouring platforms and to ground, providing beyond-line-of-sight relay across a region independent of orbital assets.

#### Typical applications

- Beyond-line-of-sight relay for dispersed units
- Mesh backhaul between ground stations
- Satellite augmentation and fallback
- Civil connectivity after infrastructure loss

### III   Programme · V-22 ApexRapid repositioning

For the situations in which the first day matters most: moving sensing and relay capacity across a region in hours.

#### The problem

Persistence is slow to establish. A stratollite takes time to reach a region and climb. When a situation develops faster than that, there is a gap between the need and the platform.

#### What we deliver

The Apex closes the gap. It carries a relay or sensor package at hypersonic speed to any region in under three and a half hours, holds a high suborbital arc as a survivable command-and-control relay, and hands over to stratollites as they arrive.

#### Typical applications

- Suborbital transit of relay and sensor payloads
- Survivable command links when satellites are unavailable
- Rapid emplacement of sensing over a developing situation

### IV   Programme · All systemsPlatform integration

Fitting a customer’s own payloads, links and ground segment to our airframes, and operating them together as one system.

#### The problem

Customers arrive with sensors they trust, links they already run, and analysts who know their own tools. An airframe that demands all of that be replaced is not useful.

#### What we deliver

Modular payload bays with documented mechanical, power and data interfaces. Ground-segment integration with existing networks. Operations training for customer teams, and joint operations from our centres in Colorado Springs, Geneva and Tokyo where that is preferred.

#### Typical applications

- Third-party sensor and payload integration
- Ground segment and data-link integration
- Operator and analyst training
- Joint or hosted operations
